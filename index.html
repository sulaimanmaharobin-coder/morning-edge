Claude traces NSA's 25-year-old elliptic-curve seeds to a plain random-number generator

An Anthropic employee used Claude to reproduce the NIST P-256 seed in about 30 minutes, making a hidden backdoor less likely.

From CoinDesk · 2026-10-17

Claude has solved a puzzle that cryptographers left open for 25 years: where the unexplained "seed" numbers behind the NIST elliptic-curve standards came from. The NSA supplied the seeds to NIST in the late 1990s, and some researchers suspected they hid a weakness.

According to CoinDesk, an Anthropic employee started the investigation with Claude. In roughly 30 minutes the model traced five NIST curve seeds to lrand48, an ordinary random-number generator from older C libraries. It reproduced the P-256 sequence and found an exact match at the 1,842nd candidate position. The result can be checked independently.

The finding makes a concealed NSA weakness significantly less likely. Bitcoin is unaffected because it uses a different, simpler curve (secp256k1) with few adjustable parameters. A $36,864 bounty tied to the puzzle was directed to the Internet Security Research Group, which runs Let's Encrypt.

Cryptographers Neal Koblitz and Alfred Menezes examined the controversy in 2015, and Steve Weis spent years on it, including FOIA requests. The new result is a concrete case of an agent doing search-and-verify work that humans had not finished.

Why it matters to youIt is a worked example of the pattern you use for overnight research agents: generate candidates, test them against a reproducible check, and report only what verifies.
